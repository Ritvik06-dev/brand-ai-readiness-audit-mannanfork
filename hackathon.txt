ADOBE UNIVERSITY HACKATHON 2026[cite: 1]
Round 3 - Build the Agent Skill Marketplace[cite: 1]

Builds on Round 2[cite: 1]
Round 2 tested reasoning on paper: why a brand is invisible / stale / bouncing in AI apps, and what to do about it[cite: 1].
Round 3 tests whether you can encode that reasoning into reusable agent skills, so that a general AI agent, pointed at any website, can audit it automatically detecting the problems that hurt both its AI discoverability (getting found and cited) and its on-site engagement (keeping the visitor once they arrive) and produce a report of findings plus suggested actions to fix them[cite: 1].

How to approach it learn from the wild[cite: 1]
We won't hand you a list of test sites[cite: 1]. Part of the challenge is field research: go find real websites that AI assistants cite well versus ones they ignore or misrepresent, and work out what makes the difference[cite: 1]. Distil those learnings - the concrete, repeatable signals that separate cited from uncited (the Round-2 failure modes) into your skill, each with evidence and a severity[cite: 1].
We won't ask which sites you studied, and none of them are used in grading[cite: 1]. Your skill is judged only on how well it generalizes to sites you've never seen (see "How submissions are evaluated" below)[cite: 1].
Design for patterns, not fit-to-examples[cite: 1]. You will be evaluated on unseen websites focus on discovering repeatable root causes, not memorizing specific site characteristics[cite: 1].

1. What you submit[cite: 1]
A single Agent Skill Marketplace: a package containing one or more skills, each authored in the standard Agent Skills (agentskills.io) format (a SKILL.md with YAML frontmatter + instructions, optional bundled scripts/ and references/), tied together by a marketplace manifest and exactly one designated entrypoint skill that receives the audit request and emits the final report[cite: 1]. Decomposing your reasoning into multiple focused skills (one per concern) is the point of the marketplace format, and is rewarded in the rubric below[cite: 1]. A marketplace with only one skill is accepted as a floor it's a valid submission, not the target[cite: 1].

Given a website, the marketplace's entrypoint skill audits it and produces an audit report containing[cite: 1]:
• Problems found the issues hurting the brand's AI discoverability and on-site engagement (the Round-2 failure modes), each with evidence and a severity[cite: 1].
• Suggested actions what to change and how to fix each problem, prioritized[cite: 1]. Suggestions may go beyond the detected problems: proactive improvements that would strengthen discoverability or engagement even where no explicit defect was found[cite: 1].

Recommend-only[cite: 1]. No skill in the marketplace modifies a live website the marketplace audits and reports[cite: 1]. Suggested actions are recommendations, not changes applied to the site[cite: 1]. No destructive, authenticated, or site-altering actions[cite: 1].

Sample audit report schema[cite: 1]
Below is the minimum required shape for the entrypoint's audit report[cite: 1]. It's a floor, not a ceiling you may add fields, but every submission must include at least these[cite: 1].

{
  "site": "example.com",
  "audited_at": "2026-09-20T14:32:00Z",
  "summary": {
    "total_findings": 6,
    "critical": 1,
    "high": 2,
    "medium": 3
  },
  "findings": [
    {
      "id": "F-001",

      "severity": "high",

        "summary": "Add Product/Offer JSON-LD to every product page.",

    }
  ]

Required fields per finding: id, title, severity, evidence, suggested_action[cite: 1]. Required summary metadata: site, audited_at, and a counts-by-severity summary[cite: 1].

Marketplace rules[cite: 1]
agentskills.io defines the single-skill SKILL.md format but does not define a multi-skill marketplace format - the manifest below is this contest's own lightweight convention[cite: 1]. Every skill folder inside your marketplace must still independently satisfy the official agentskills.io spec[cite: 1].

• Every skill folder listed in the marketplace must contain a valid SKILL.md per the agentskills.io spec (name, description, etc.)[cite: 1]. If you have Python/npm available, you can sanity-check with skills-ref validate ./skill-folder not required, just a convenience[cite: 1].
• The marketplace root must include a top-level manifest, marketplace.json, listing every skill and marking exactly one as the entrypoint[cite: 1]. The entrypoint is the skill we invoke; it's responsible for composing the outputs of any other skills in the marketplace into the single audit report described above[cite: 1].

3. How submissions are evaluated[cite: 1]
We evaluate the submitted marketplace itself its skills' instructions, checks, logic, and how they're composed by the entrypoint not any single report it happens to produce[cite: 1]. We look for:
• What problems it is built to identify does the skill encode the right checks to surface the real causes of poor AI discoverability and engagement, with evidence and without false positives?[cite: 1]
• How it addresses them does the skill produce fixes that are correct, specific, mechanism-sound, and that actually resolve the problems it finds?[cite: 1]
• How the marketplace is composed - if you split the work across multiple skills, does the decomposition reflect genuine separation of concerns and does the entrypoint compose them cleanly, or is it padding? A single well-built skill is not penalized for not decomposing[cite: 1].
• What it does to improve AI discoverability & engagement beyond fixing specific defects - does it recommend actions that genuinely strengthen how the brand is found, cited, and experienced?[cite: 1]
• Generalization no example sites are provided during the task, so generalization is tested by construction[cite: 1].

5. Scope & guardrails[cite: 1]
• Recommend-only the marketplace audits and reports; no skill in it ever alters a live site, and everything runs read-only in a sandbox[cite: 1].
• No destructive, authenticated-area, or rate-abusing actions[cite: 1]. Respect robots.txt[cite: 1].

Appendix - Background Concepts (from Round 2)[cite: 1]
This appendix explains the general ideas behind why AI assistants find, trust, and repeat some content and not others[cite: 1]. It describes how these systems behave not what to do about any particular site[cite: 1]. Working out the checks and fixes is your job[cite: 1].

A. How search visibility works (the basics)[cite: 1]. Search engines, and increasingly AI assistants, discover content the same basic way: an automated program (a "crawler") visits a page, reads what's there, and stores it so it can be found later[cite: 1]. For a page to be visible at all, three things have to succeed in order the crawler has to be let in, it has to be able to read what's on the page, and it has to be able to pick out the specific fact someone is looking for[cite: 1]. If any one of those steps fails, the page effectively doesn't exist for that system - plainly visible to a human, yet invisible to the machine[cite: 1].

B. How assistants like ChatGPT use sources[cite: 1]. When you ask an assistant a question, it doesn't always answer purely from memory[cite: 1]. Many assistants can go and look things up in the moment searching, fetching a few pages, and building the answer from what those pages happen to say, sometimes showing links back to them[cite: 1]. What ends up in the answer depends on what the assistant can find and use at that moment: the pages that get picked as sources tend to be the ones a machine could easily reach, easily read, and easily quote a clear fact from[cite: 1]. If nothing about a brand is easy to reach, read, and quote, it simply won't make it into the answer at all[cite: 1].

C. How machines "read" a page[cite: 1]. A page that looks complete to a person isn't always complete to a machine[cite: 1]. Some content is only assembled after a page loads, or lives in a form a simple reader can't interpret so a fact that's plainly visible on screen can be invisible to a program that isn't looking at it the same way[cite: 1]. In general, the more explicitly and unambiguously a fact is stated in plain, readable text, the more likely a machine is to extract it correctly; the more it's implied, buried, or locked inside something non-textual, the more likely it's missed[cite: 1].

D. Why agreement across the web matters[cite: 1]. Machines tend to treat a fact as more trustworthy when many independent places say the same thing[cite: 1]. A claim that lives in only one spot is fragile; a claim repeated consistently across lots of unrelated sources is far more likely to be believed and repeated back[cite: 1]. A related problem is mistaken identity: when several different things share a name, a system can mix them up unless there's something that clearly distinguishes one from the others[cite: 1]. So how a brand is described across the wider web not just on its own pages shapes what assistants say about it[cite: 1].

E. Personalization and prior context[cite: 1]. These assistants don't treat every user identically[cite: 1]. They can draw on context they already hold about a person - earlier messages in the conversation, past questions, stated preferences, location, or other signals and use it to tailor what they surface, so the answer is weighted toward what seems most relevant to that user[cite: 1]. This is one more reason two people can ask the very same question and get different brands, framing, or emphasis in return: part of the answer depends not just on the content that exists out there, but on who's asking[cite: 1].

F. Why machines drop content from emails[cite: 1]. Inboxes increasingly show short AI-generated summaries of long messages[cite: 1]. Those summaries are built from the text of a message[cite: 1]. When the real substance of an email isn't available as readable text for example, when it's carried by something a summarizer can't read, or when the genuinely important lines are surrounded by low-value filler the summary has little to work with, and the important part can simply disappear[cite: 1].
